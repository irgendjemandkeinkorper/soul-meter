## 3. About This Game

> **Everything worth doing costs you something.**
>
> Not gold. Not time. You.
>
> Soul Meter is a turn-based CRPG about a currency you cannot farm. Talk a marshal out of a hanging
> and it costs you. Keep a promise you should not have made and it costs you. The meter goes down
> across a chapter, and the only thing that puts anything back is an act of Agreement — a promise
> kept, a companion's private ruin resolved well, a place spoken back into accord. There is no rest
> mechanic. There are no potions. There is only what you were willing to do, and what you have
> left afterwards.
>
> Run out and you do not die. You **hollow**. Dialogue options close. Companions get quieter.
> Strangers start reading you as Waning-touched before you have said a word. It is recoverable, at
> a price, and it is never a game over — which is worse.
>
> **A world that writes things down.**
>
> Sixty factions keep standing with you, independently, and they do not forget. Above them sits a
> second ledger: what you are known for, and what you are notorious for. Steal from the wrong
> pocket in Dom and the Registry hears about it before you have crossed the square. Every entry
> names the act that caused it, so you can always find out exactly which version of yourself the
> room is reacting to.
>
> **Combat that is not a formality.**
>
> Turn-based tactical fights on a grid, ordered by charge time rather than rounds, so speed is a
> real investment and waiting is a real move. Weather rides the same clock the initiative order
> does — fight a bog-wight in the mozh and the ground argues with you. The forecast you are shown
> before you commit is the same calculation that resolves the action; the game does not lie to you
> about your odds, and it does not reroll behind your back.
>
> **Character, not build.**
>
> Ten patron classes decide what you *are*. Seven attributes and a spread of skills decide what you
> can *do* with it. They are separate on purpose: a warrior can pour everything into archive work
> and carry weaker class abilities, and the game will let you, and the consequences are yours.
> Attributes are fixed at creation. Skills are where you grow. Locks, pockets, prices, and what is
> hidden in the bottom of a crate are all skill checks — the field is not a corridor between fights.
>
> **Companions with their own reasons.**
>
> Twenty recruits, two for each patron class, drinking in the same tavern. You take two. Each has
> a personal quest that resolves well, badly, or not at all, and the difference is usually
> something you said several hours earlier.
>
> **Chapter One of the Dramgid Cycle.** Era 3. The world is thinning, the Trials still sit, and the
> front is coming whether anyone is ready or not.

## 4. Feature bullets

Steam renders these as a short list. Six, ordered by what is most distinctive:

- **A soul you spend, not a resource you farm.** Meaningful acts cost soul. Only acts of Agreement
  return it. The meter trends down across a chapter, by design.
- **Hollowing, not dying.** Empty is a *state*, not a failure screen — closed dialogue, changed
  reactions, a different game rather than a shorter one.
- **Two ledgers, sixty factions.** Standing is per-faction and append-only; every entry names its
  cause. A second, world-wide ledger tracks renown and infamy separately.
- **Charge-time tactical combat.** Grid fights ordered by charge time, with live weather and terrain
  charge. The forecast is the resolution — same numbers, no hidden rerolls.
- **Skills that work outside combat.** Locked containers, pickpocketing, barter that actually moves
  the price, and loot worth searching for.
- **Ten patron classes, seven attributes.** Class is identity; attributes and skills are capability.
  The build never tells you who you are.

## 5. Scope honesty (put this on the page, not in the fine print)

A paid Chapter One earns a review score for what it is, not for what is planned. The store page
should say so plainly and early — one short block near the bottom of §3:

> **This is Chapter One.** It is a complete chapter with its own resolution, not a demo and not an
> early-access shell. It is also the first of a longer cycle, and it ends where a chapter ends.
> Expect roughly a chapter's worth of play, one city and the roads out of it, and a ruling at the
> end that the next chapter will hold you to.

Do **not** put a play-time number on the page until round-1 playtest (#93) reports one. The owner's
20-hour figure is a goal, not a measurement, and a number on the store page becomes a promise the
reviews will hold you to.

## 9. Capsule brief

Owner produces via the S2 prompt bible (`docs/art-aesthetics-bible.md`); this is the brief, not the
prompt.

**Subject:** one figure, seen from behind or three-quarter, small against a thinning landscape.
Not a hero pose. The reading should be *someone who has already paid for something*.

**What the capsule must carry at 231×87 (the smallest size Steam shows):** the title, and one
strong silhouette. Nothing else survives that size — no party shot, no UI, no legible detail.

**Palette:** the DS tokens — parchment, ash, cinder for the single accent. The accent should appear
exactly once, and it should be the thing the eye lands on.

**Avoid:** glowing weapons, a party lineup, anything that reads as high fantasy heroics, and any
figure making eye contact with the viewer. The tone is elegiac; a confident stare sells a different
game.
